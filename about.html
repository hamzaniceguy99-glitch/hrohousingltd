How we work

Why licensing comes before letting

Houses in multiple occupation are licensed because of what concentrates in them: more occupants, shared escape routes, shared amenities, and a higher fire risk than a single household home. Mandatory licensing applies where five or more people in two or more households share an amenity, and local authorities may designate additional or selective licensing schemes covering smaller properties or particular areas. Birmingham has done so, which makes this a live question across much of the city rather than an edge case.

The penalty structure is what makes it the sharpest rule in private renting. Operating a licensable property without a licence is an offence that can bring an unlimited fine on conviction, or a financial penalty imposed directly by the authority. Separately, a rent repayment order can require the landlord to repay up to twelve months' rent, on the application of either a tenant or the local authority. And while the property is required to be licensed and is not, no section 21 notice can validly be served — so possession is unavailable for the entire unlicensed period.

A licence also comes with conditions, the most consequential being the maximum permitted number of occupants. Treating that as a target to reach rather than a limit not to exceed is how a licensed house becomes an unlicensed one in substance. So the licence is obtained before anyone moves in, the occupancy limit is observed even when a room sits empty, and the specific duties in the HMO management regulations — displayed manager details, clear means of escape, maintained fire precautions, common parts in repair — are treated as the checkable obligations they are rather than good practice.
